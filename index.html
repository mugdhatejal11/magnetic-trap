Three loops, three speeds

Think of the simulation as nested loops. The innermost one is so fast that, at true scale, its circle is smaller than a pixel, so the spiral is drawn magnified.

Inner loop: spin 0 The field forces the particle into tiny circles around a field line. This is the tick of the whole system.
Middle loop: bounce 0round trips While spinning, it slides along the line toward a pole, turns around, and comes back. Like a request that retries between two servers.
Outer loop: drift 0.00° The whole bouncing pattern slowly creeps around the Earth. You only see it move at Warp speed.

Why it turns around

Think of the particle's speed as a fixed budget split between two threads: moving forward along the field line, and spinning sideways. The field never adds speed. It only reallocates it. Near the poles the field is stronger, and a stronger field pulls more of the budget into spinning. When the forward share hits zero, there is nothing left to move forward with, so the particle reverses.

The update rule, and three ways to run it

This is the entire engine. Everything you see comes from repeating three lines.

# every step:
B = field_at(position)             # look up the field where the particle is
velocity = rotate(velocity, B)     # the field turns the velocity; it never lengthens it
position = position + velocity * dt
BorisImplements that middle line literally, as a rotation. A rotation cannot change speed, so the conservation law holds by construction.
RK4The textbook high-accuracy solver. It doesn't know the rule, so it leaks speed slowly, and it becomes unstable if the step is too big for the spin.
EulerReplaces the rotation with a small sideways nudge. Each nudge adds a sliver of speed, and the slivers compound.

Unit tests running live

Three assertions, checked as the simulation runs. The first two need no answer key, only the rule. The third compares the run to a formula.

WaitingSpeed is constant-A magnetic field does no work, so the total speed must never change.
WaitingHidden invariant holds-The quantity (sideways speed)² ÷ (field strength) should stay nearly constant. This is what forces the turnaround.
WaitingTurning point matches theory-
Show me the math: two clean graphs and three equations
1. μ = v⊥² / 2B stays constant along the path
2. at the turning point v⊥ = v, so B(turn) / B₀ = 1 / sin²α₀
3. on a dipole field line B(λ) / B₀ = √(1 + 3 sin²λ) / cos⁶λ

v⊥ is the sideways speed, α₀ is the pitch angle at the equator, B₀ is the field strength at the equator crossing, and λ is latitude. Equations 2 and 3 together give one number: the latitude where the particle turns around.

Where the turning point comes from

The curve is equation 3. The horizontal line is equation 2. Where they cross is the turning point. If that crossing is to the right of the dashed Earth line, it is underground, and the particle hits the atmosphere.

What carries over to software

A conserved quantity is a free unit testThe speed check above never needs the correct answer, only the rule. If your simulation breaks an invariant, it is wrong even when the animation looks plausible.
The step must fit the fastest loopThe spin is hundreds of times faster than the bounce, so the spin sets the step size, not the thing you care about. Same story as a stiff system or a tick rate that is too low.
Separated time scales can be collapsedBecause the spin is so fast and regular, physicists track only the center of the circle and treat the spin as a summary. It's the same trade as caching or batching an inner loop.

The field shape, the turning points and the loss cone are real physics. The particle is a stand-in: its charge-to-mass ratio is chosen so the spin is much tighter than the field's curvature (which is what makes the textbook theory apply), distances are in Earth radii, and time is in arbitrary units, so the absolute speeds are not those of a real proton or electron.
